# 30 · Inventory Turns and Shelf-Life Optimization

> **Part:** [[C - Operations]]  ·  **Index:** [[How to Analyze a Consumer Packaged Goods Company]]

## 🎯 Goal
The goal of an Inventory Turns and Shelf-Life Optimization analysis in the consumer packaged goods (CPG) industry is to evaluate and enhance how efficiently inventory is managed, specifically for perishable and limited-shelf-life products. The analysis aims to balance inventory levels with demand to minimize waste, reduce stockouts, improve product freshness, and optimize working capital. Inventory turns indicate how often inventory is sold and replaced over a specific period, while shelf-life optimization focuses on ensuring that products are sold before they expire or lose freshness.


## 📊 Data required
1. Sales data by SKU, including sales velocity and demand trends
2. Inventory levels at distribution centers, warehouses, and retail locations
3. Shelf-life data for each SKU (e.g., expiration dates, freshness windows)
4. Stockout frequency and lost sales data due to insufficient inventory
5. Spoilage and waste data for perishable or time-sensitive products
6. Supplier lead times and order cycle data
7. Inventory holding costs, including storage, insurance, and spoilage costs
8. Safety stock requirements and reorder points for each SKU
9. Demand forecasting data, including seasonality and promotional impact
10. Competitor benchmarking data for inventory turnover rates and shelf-life management


## 🪜 Step-by-step

1. **Define the objectives of the analysis:** Set clear goals for inventory turns and shelf-life optimization. Objectives may include increasing inventory turnover, reducing waste from expired products, improving product freshness, and optimizing storage costs. For example, a goal might be to improve inventory turnover by 15% for perishable products while maintaining shelf availability and minimizing spoilage.
2. **Calculate current inventory turnover:** Inventory turnover is a key performance indicator that measures how many times inventory is sold and replaced over a period. The formula for inventory turnover is:
`Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) / Average Inventory For example, if a SKU has $500,000 in COGS and an average inventory value of $200,000: Inventory Turnover = $500,000 / $200,000 = 2.5 This means the inventory is turned over 2.5 times in a given period. A higher turnover ratio indicates more efficient inventory management, while a low ratio suggests that stock may be sitting too long.`
3. **Analyze inventory levels and shelf-life data:** Review current inventory levels for each SKU and cross-reference them with product shelf-life data. Ensure that the stock levels are aligned with demand forecasts and that products are sold within their freshness window. For perishable items, it’s critical to keep inventory turnover high to avoid spoilage or the sale of expired products. Shelf-life utilization can be measured by tracking how much of the product’s shelf life is used before it reaches the consumer. Optimizing shelf life helps ensure that products are still fresh when purchased.
4. **Monitor spoilage and waste data:** Track the amount of waste or spoilage due to expired products. The formula for spoilage rate is:
`Spoilage Rate (%) = (Quantity of Spoiled Goods / Total Quantity of Goods) x 100 For example, if 300 units out of 5,000 spoil: Spoilage Rate = (300 / 5,000) x 100 = 6%`
179 Reducing spoilage requires optimizing inventory levels, improving demand forecasting, and adjusting safety stock levels to match shelf-life constraints.
5. **Align safety stock with demand and shelf-life requirements:** Review safety stock levels to ensure they are aligned with demand variability and shelf-life limits. Holding excess safety stock for products with short shelf lives can lead to waste, while insufficient stock increases the risk of stockouts and lost sales. Calculate safety stock based on demand fluctuations and lead time variability to strike the right balance.
6. **Evaluate supplier lead times and order cycles:** Assess how supplier lead times impact inventory turnover and shelf life. Long lead times or infrequent orders may result in holding excess stock to avoid stockouts, but this can increase the risk of spoilage for perishable goods. Work with suppliers to optimize order cycles, reduce lead times, and ensure frequent replenishment.
7. **Use demand forecasting to optimize inventory levels:** Analyze historical sales data, seasonality, and promotional events to create accurate demand forecasts. This allows for better alignment between inventory levels and actual demand, reducing excess stock and the risk of product expiration. Adjust forecasts for seasonal products or time-sensitive promotional periods to avoid overstocking or understocking.
8. **Implement a first-in, first-out (FIFO) system for perishable goods:** Ensure that older stock is sold first by using a FIFO system in warehouses and retail locations. This reduces the likelihood of expired products sitting in inventory and helps maintain product freshness. FIFO systems are particularly important for managing inventory with short shelf lives, such as dairy, meats, or produce.
9. **Monitor stockout rates and lost sales:** Track the frequency of stockouts and the associated lost sales. Stockouts for high-demand products can lead to lost revenue and customer dissatisfaction, while overstocking leads to waste. Optimizing inventory turnover reduces the risk of both stockouts and excess inventory, especially for products with limited shelf life.
180
10. **Benchmark against competitors:** Compare your inventory turnover rates, spoilage rates, and shelf-life management strategies with industry competitors. Benchmarking helps identify areas for improvement, such as reducing waste or increasing inventory turnover, and provides insight into best practices for managing time-sensitive products.


## 📤 Output format
The output of an Inventory Turns and Shelf-Life Optimization analysis typically includes:
- Inventory turnover rates by SKU, product category, and location
- Spoilage rates and waste analysis for perishable or time-sensitive products
- Current inventory levels and shelf-life data, highlighting products at risk of expiration
- Supplier lead time and order cycle analysis, with recommendations for improvements
- Safety stock analysis to ensure alignment with demand and shelf-life requirements
- Demand forecasting data and alignment with inventory levels and replenishment schedules
- Recommendations for optimizing inventory management, reducing waste, and improving turnover
- Competitor benchmarking data on inventory turns and perishability management


## 🔍 How to interpret the results
- **Low inventory turnover with high spoilage rates:** This indicates inefficiencies in inventory management, likely due to overstocking or poor demand forecasting.
181 Adjust inventory levels to match demand more closely and improve turnover to reduce waste from expired products.
- **High stockout rates with lost sales:** Frequent stockouts suggest that inventory levels are too low or that demand forecasting is inaccurate. Improving demand forecasting and adjusting safety stock levels can help reduce stockouts and prevent lost sales.
- **Long supplier lead times with excess inventory:** If lead times are long and inventory levels are high, this may result in holding more stock than necessary, increasing the risk of spoilage. Work with suppliers to shorten lead times or implement more frequent deliveries to avoid overstocking.
- **Excessive safety stock for short-shelf-life products:** If safety stock levels are too high for products with limited shelf life, this could lead to waste. Recalculate safety stock to ensure that it balances demand fluctuations with the risk of spoilage.


## 🚀 Steps to improve
1. **Improve demand forecasting accuracy:** Use historical sales data, seasonality, and promotional trends to create more accurate demand forecasts. This ensures that inventory levels are aligned with actual consumer demand, reducing both stockouts and waste.
2. **Implement a FIFO inventory management system:** Ensure that older stock is sold first by using FIFO methods in both warehouses and retail locations. This helps reduce the likelihood of products expiring before they are sold and keeps inventory fresh.
3. **Collaborate with suppliers to optimize lead times:** Work with suppliers to reduce lead times and increase delivery frequency. Shorter lead times allow for better inventory control, reducing the need for excess safety stock and minimizing the risk of spoilage.
182
4. **Reduce excess inventory for short-shelf-life products:** Adjust inventory levels for perishable products to ensure they are in line with demand. Excess inventory of perishable items increases the risk of waste, so optimizing inventory levels is critical to minimizing losses.
5. **Track and improve inventory turnover rates:** Continuously monitor inventory turnover rates and aim to increase them, especially for products with limited shelf life. Higher turnover rates reduce the risk of products sitting in inventory too long and expiring.
6. **Optimize safety stock based on shelf-life constraints:** Recalculate safety stock levels for perishable goods, taking into account demand variability and shelf-life constraints. Balancing safety stock with product freshness helps reduce waste while maintaining availability.
